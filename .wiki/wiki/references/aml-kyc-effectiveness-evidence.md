# AML and KYC Effectiveness Evidence

## Bottom line

The evidence does not support either “AML/KYC works” or “AML/KYC is useless” as a general proposition.

- **Well supported:** reporting systems produce financial intelligence that investigators use; supervision can change reporting behaviour; freezing and confiscation occur.
- **Partly supported:** rules alter institutional and market behaviour, including reporting and capital-flow responses.
- **Weakly supported:** causal, cost-effective reduction in total laundering, predicate crime or social harm.
- **Documented but incompletely quantified:** compliance cost, de-risking, account exclusion, correspondent-bank withdrawal, displacement and privacy burdens.

## Measurement chain

| Stage | Example | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| input | law, KYC system, staff, IT, supervision, cost | useful or accurate output |
| compliance output | alert, review, SAR/STR, account restriction | confirmed crime or useful investigation |
| investigative output | FIU dissemination, lead, case, seizure | conviction, final confiscation or deterrence |
| enforcement output | conviction, final confiscation | aggregate reduction in crime or net social benefit |
| social outcome | less laundering, higher offender cost, less predicate harm | proportionate distribution of costs and rights burdens |
| side effect | exclusion, de-risking, informal-channel displacement | failure of every covered measure |

An alert is not a suspicious-transaction report; a report is not a criminal case; a case is not a conviction. Growth at an earlier stage cannot be treated as proof at a later stage.

## Evidence matrix

| Source and measure | Narrow finding | Essential limit |
|---|---|---|
| IMF 1998 | described 2–5 percent of world GDP as a possible contemporary consensus range | not a measurement; the speaker disclaimed accuracy; not a current annual estimate |
| UNODC 2011, model for 2009 | estimated criminal proceeds at 3.6 percent of world GDP, about 2.7 percent available for laundering, and much less than one percent—probably about 0.2 percent—of proceeds laundered through the financial system seized or frozen | modelled denominator; not all criminal proceeds; seizure/freezing is not final confiscation; historical order of magnitude |
| Europol 2016, 2010–2014 EU data | estimated 2.2 percent of criminal proceeds frozen/seized and 1.1 percent finally confiscated | incomplete national data and modelled proceeds; asset recovery is not the whole prevention effect |
| GAO 2020, survey of 5,257 federal personnel | more than 72 percent reported using BSA data, often to identify subjects or expand cases | self-reported operational use, not a crime-reduction estimate |
| GAO 2020, non-generalisable sample of eleven banks | 2018 direct BSA costs ranged from about USD 14,000 to USD 21 million; roughly two percent of operating costs at each of the three smallest and below one percent at each of the three largest | no national or global extrapolation; direct costs only |
| Bank of Italy, 736 banks | inspections associated with about 18 percent more STRs without measured average quality decline | possible inspection selection; FIU score and report volume are outputs, not offences prevented |
| Europol 2017, almost one million 2014 reports | about ten percent were further investigated; reporting was highly concentrated in two Member States | heterogeneous regimes and resource choices; not a ten-percent precision or ninety-percent false-positive rate |
| FATF 2022, 120 fourth-round evaluations published by December 2021 | high/substantial effectiveness: 28 percent for IO6, 9 percent for IO7 and 19 percent for IO8 | assessor ratings for separate Immediate Outcomes, not causal impact; later follow-up did not update these effectiveness ratings |
| Hock et al. evidence review | no reviewed study met the authors' minimum standard for linking a prevention programme to lower laundering risk | shows an evaluation gap, not proof that programmes have no effect |
| Nazzari and Reuter 2025 | operational investigation benefits coexist with weak evidence of aggregate reduction or systematically higher offender cost | critical narrative synthesis, not a causal meta-analysis |
| EBA 2022 | de-risking affects identified sectors and categories; blanket exclusion can be unjustified | no comprehensive data to quantify EU prevalence or isolate AML from other causes |
| IMF 2021 working paper | grey-listing associated with a 7.6-percent-of-GDP fall in capital inflows | limited treated observations, non-random listing and possible unobserved confounding |
| FATF Austria 2026 | only moderate effectiveness for financial intelligence, ML investigation/prosecution and confiscation, despite material infrastructure | country diagnosis, not the counterfactual of Austria without AML controls |

## Numbers that require correction

1. **“Two to five percent of global GDP is laundered each year.”** The range traces to a cautious 1998 IMF speech, not an observed or current statistic. UNODC later modelled 2009 at about 2.7 percent available for laundering, with major uncertainty.
2. **“Less than one percent is recovered.”** UNODC's key finding estimated much less than one percent—probably about 0.2 percent—of proceeds laundered through the financial system seized or frozen. That is not all criminal proceeds or final confiscation. Europol separately estimated provisional and final EU figures for 2010–2014.
3. **“Ninety percent of STRs are false positives.”** Europol's approximately ten-percent further-investigation figure is not ground truth or precision. A report can be duplicate, intelligence-relevant, below an investigative threshold or unexamined because of resources.
4. **“Only nineteen percent of AML systems are effective.”** FATF's 19 percent concerns IO8 confiscation ratings; IO6 and IO7 have different values.
5. **“Compliance costs exceed recovered assets by a fixed multiple.”** Global cost estimates and modelled crime/recovery denominators are generally not comparable enough for that conclusion.

## Benefits and counterevidence

The evidence against a categorical failure claim includes:

- substantial self-reported use of BSA reports within the six surveyed US federal agencies;
- identification of new subjects and links;
- real seizures and final confiscations;
- reporting responses to supervision without a measured average fall in the selected quality proxy;
- institutional reporting and market responses showing that controls can alter behaviour, without quantifying net prevention.

The missing step is a defensible counterfactual: how much laundering, predicate crime and harm would have occurred without the relevant control, and at what total cost and rights burden?

## Side effects

De-risking and financial exclusion are recognised by the EBA and FATF itself. They are not fully quantified and may also arise from sanctions, profitability, capital, reputation or operational constraints. FATF's 2025 proportionality changes expressly encourage simplified measures at lower risk and attention to over-compliance.

Grey-listing illustrates de facto pressure. FATF does not legally demand blanket de-risking against grey-listed countries, yet the IMF working paper found a large associated capital-flow decline.

## Relevance to crypto and DAC8

General AML evidence can inform the public-interest and suitability analysis but cannot by itself establish the proportionality or effectiveness of a specific crypto or tax-reporting duty.

- KYC/CDD can create identity data and investigative chokepoints.
- That utility does not prove the incremental need for every field, customer category, retention period or onward transfer.
- DAC8 primarily serves tax transparency. AML asset-recovery or STR evidence is not an outcome measure for DAC8.
- Weak asset-recovery ratios likewise do not prove that DAC8 or crypto KYC is invalid or useless.

## Claims to avoid

- “AML has been proven effective.”
- “AML has been proven ineffective.”
- “Ninety percent of reports are false.”
- “Less than one percent proves that 99 percent of AML fails.”
- “FATF ratings measure crime reduction.”
- “De-risking is caused only by AML.”
- “General AML evidence proves the effect of DAC8.”
