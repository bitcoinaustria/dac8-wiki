# AML and KYC Effectiveness Evidence

## Bottom line

The evidence does not support either “AML/KYC works” or “AML/KYC is useless” as a general proposition.

- **Well supported:** reporting systems produce financial intelligence that investigators use; supervision can change reporting behaviour; freezing and confiscation occur.
- **Partly supported:** rules alter institutional and market behaviour, including reporting and capital-flow responses.
- **Weakly supported:** causal, cost-effective reduction in total laundering, predicate crime or social harm.
- **Documented but incompletely quantified:** compliance cost, de-risking, account exclusion, correspondent-bank withdrawal, displacement and privacy burdens.
- **Documented by official findings:** formal KYC/AML programmes coexisting with years of unmonitored or uncontrolled flows (TD Bank, Danske Bank Estonia), and verified accounts held under stolen, forged or synthetic identities (A-FIU 2024, FinCEN 2024, Eurojust 2026).
- **Officially conceded:** the Commission's 2021 impact assessment states that AML's effect on laundering "can only be indirectly perceived via proxy indicators" that "can be misleading"; the EU had no published ML/TF statistics when the Court of Auditors reported in 2021.

## Measurement chain

| Stage | Example | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| input | law, KYC system, staff, IT, supervision, cost | useful or accurate output |
| compliance output | alert, review, SAR/STR, account restriction | confirmed crime or useful investigation |
| investigative output | FIU dissemination, lead, case, seizure | conviction, final confiscation or deterrence |
| enforcement output | conviction, final confiscation | aggregate reduction in crime or net social benefit |
| social outcome | less laundering, higher offender cost, less predicate harm | proportionate distribution of costs and rights burdens |
| side effect | exclusion, de-risking, informal-channel displacement | failure of every covered measure |

An alert is not a suspicious-transaction report; a report is not a criminal case; a case is not a conviction. Growth at an earlier stage cannot be treated as proof at a later stage.

## Evidence matrix

| Source and measure | Narrow finding | Essential limit |
|---|---|---|
| IMF 1998 | described 2–5 percent of world GDP as a possible contemporary consensus range | not a measurement; the speaker disclaimed accuracy; not a current annual estimate |
| UNODC 2011, model for 2009 | estimated criminal proceeds at 3.6 percent of world GDP, about 2.7 percent available for laundering, and much less than one percent—probably about 0.2 percent—of proceeds laundered through the financial system seized or frozen | modelled denominator; not all criminal proceeds; seizure/freezing is not final confiscation; historical order of magnitude |
| Europol 2016, 2010–2014 EU data | estimated 2.2 percent of criminal proceeds frozen/seized and 1.1 percent finally confiscated | incomplete national data and modelled proceeds; asset recovery is not the whole prevention effect |
| GAO 2020, survey of 5,257 federal personnel | more than 72 percent reported using BSA data, often to identify subjects or expand cases | self-reported operational use, not a crime-reduction estimate |
| GAO 2020, non-generalisable sample of eleven banks | 2018 direct BSA costs ranged from about USD 14,000 to USD 21 million; roughly two percent of operating costs at each of the three smallest and below one percent at each of the three largest | no national or global extrapolation; direct costs only |
| Bank of Italy, 736 banks | inspections associated with about 18 percent more STRs without measured average quality decline | possible inspection selection; FIU score and report volume are outputs, not offences prevented |
| Europol 2017, almost one million 2014 reports | about ten percent were further investigated; reporting was highly concentrated in two Member States | heterogeneous regimes and resource choices; not a ten-percent precision or ninety-percent false-positive rate |
| FATF 2022, 120 fourth-round evaluations published by December 2021 | high/substantial effectiveness: 28 percent for IO6, 9 percent for IO7 and 19 percent for IO8 | assessor ratings for separate Immediate Outcomes, not causal impact; later follow-up did not update these effectiveness ratings |
| Hock et al. evidence review | no reviewed study met the authors' minimum standard for linking a prevention programme to lower laundering risk | shows an evaluation gap, not proof that programmes have no effect |
| Nazzari and Reuter 2025 | operational investigation benefits coexist with weak evidence of aggregate reduction or systematically higher offender cost | critical narrative synthesis, not a causal meta-analysis |
| EBA 2022 | de-risking affects identified sectors and categories; blanket exclusion can be unjustified | no comprehensive data to quantify EU prevalence or isolate AML from other causes |
| IMF 2021 working paper | grey-listing associated with a 7.6-percent-of-GDP fall in capital inflows | limited treated observations, non-random listing and possible unobserved confounding |
| FATF Austria 2026 | only moderate effectiveness for financial intelligence, ML investigation/prosecution and confiscation, despite material infrastructure | country diagnosis, not the counterfactual of Austria without AML controls |
| Pol 2020, peer-reviewed policy analysis | about 0.1 percent of criminal proceeds intercepted (0.07–0.16 percent by input); compliance cost about USD 304 billion globally and about 120 times recoveries in the EU (sections 6.1–6.4) | author states data are "poorly validated" and findings "cannot be definitive"; cost side extrapolates commercial surveys by GDP; author has an AML-assurance affiliation |
| Commission impact assessment SWD(2021) 190 | effect on laundering only visible through misleading proxies; relays bank estimates that 50–75 percent of STRs are of no significance; acknowledges defensive over-reporting | the 50–75 percent figure is confidential, unaudited industry input; the document argues for harmonisation, not against CDD |
| European Court of Auditors, Special Report 13/2021 | fragmented EU implementation; transposition checks of 20–40 months; ML/TF statistics not published; one EBA breach-of-Union-law finding since 2010 (paras 48, 54, 119) | governance audit; measures neither laundering nor national CDD; predates AMLR, AMLA and MiCA/TFR |
| A-FIU Lagebericht 2024 (Austria) | 11,111 STRs (1,228 from VASPs); 27 percent disseminated, 8,138 retained for passive analysis; about EUR 20 million secured; 152 final ML convictions | no cohort tracking from report to outcome; convictions not linked to reports or to VASP reports; no cost data |
| German FIU Jahresbericht 2025 | 374,693 STRs; of 41,743 prosecutor feedback notices, 1,669 concerned judgments, penalty orders, decisions or indictments; about 96 percent were discontinuations; EUR 751.8 million secured through 106 immediate measures (EUR 585 million from one case) | feedback notices are not a cohort; discontinuation does not mean the information was useless; a comparator, not Austrian evidence |
| UKFIU SARs Annual Report 2024-25 | 866,616 SARs, 1.67 percent from virtual-asset firms; GBP 382.6 million denied through consent (DAML) requests, GBP 103 million from one case | UK consent regime has no Austrian equivalent; no outcome measure for ordinary SARs; denial includes temporary restraint |
| TD Bank 2024 (DOJ and FinCEN, admitted facts) | 92 percent of transaction volume, about USD 18.3 trillion, unmonitored in 2018–2024; monitoring static 2014–2022; over USD 670 million moved by three networks, partly with employee help | institution-level failure, later detected and penalised; not a system-level effectiveness estimate |
| Danske Bank Estonia (DOJ 2022 plea; Bruun & Hjejle 2018) | USD 160 billion processed through US banks; about EUR 200 billion non-resident flow 2007–2015; "all control functions (or lines of defence) had failed" | the flow is not proven laundering; the report itself separates "suspicious" from criminal |

## Numbers that require correction

1. **“Two to five percent of global GDP is laundered each year.”** The range traces to a cautious 1998 IMF speech, not an observed or current statistic. UNODC later modelled 2009 at about 2.7 percent available for laundering, with major uncertainty.
2. **“Less than one percent is recovered.”** UNODC's key finding estimated much less than one percent—probably about 0.2 percent—of proceeds laundered through the financial system seized or frozen. That is not all criminal proceeds or final confiscation. Europol separately estimated provisional and final EU figures for 2010–2014.
3. **“Ninety percent of STRs are false positives.”** Europol's approximately ten-percent further-investigation figure is not ground truth or precision. A report can be duplicate, intelligence-relevant, below an investigative threshold or unexamined because of resources. The same limit applies to the A-FIU's 27 percent dissemination rate, the German four-percent share of court-stage feedback and the Commission's relayed 50–75 percent “of no significance”: none is a measured false-positive rate. No official alert-to-report or report-to-outcome precision rate was found.
4. **“Only nineteen percent of AML systems are effective.”** FATF's 19 percent concerns IO8 confiscation ratings; IO6 and IO7 have different values.
5. **“Compliance costs exceed recovered assets by a fixed multiple.”** Global cost estimates and modelled crime/recovery denominators are generally not comparable enough for that conclusion. Pol's “more than a hundred times” may be quoted only as his estimate, with his own caveats, and not as a measured ratio.
6. **“EUR 200 billion was laundered through Danske Bank.”** EUR 200 billion is the total non-resident flow examined for 2007–2015. The bank's investigators stated there was no accurate estimate of the suspicious share. The Commission's 2021 “EUR 200 billion in suspicious transactions” is itself a loose reading.

## Austrian suspicion-to-outcome chain

The A-FIU 2024 report allows a partial Austrian chain:

| Stage | 2024 figure | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| reports | 11,111 STRs; banks 9,704, VASPs 1,228 | new goAML counting; earlier years not directly comparable |
| quality return | 9 percent of first-time reports returned for improvement | internal quality measure |
| dissemination | 2,973 analysis reports (27 percent); about 2 percent to tax or customs authorities | not a measure of whether a report was correct or useful |
| retention | 8,138 reports kept for passive future analysis for lack of initial suspicion or further analytical leads | retained data remain available for matching |
| asset denial | about EUR 20 million secured | not final confiscation |
| convictions | 152 final ML convictions (81 to 120 in 2020–2023) | not linked to specific reports; convictions for predicate offences not counted |

Austria publishes no prosecutor-feedback breakdown comparable to Germany's § 42 GwG statistics. FATF's 2026 moderate ratings for financial intelligence, investigation and confiscation are consistent with this chain but do not quantify it. The report is adverse to a claim that the Austrian system produces nothing; it does not establish what share of reports, or of VASP reports, contributes to an outcome.

## Formal compliance versus control quality

TD Bank and Danske Bank Estonia show that a programme can exist on paper while controls are absent in practice. DOJ described TD Bank's programme as one that “appeared adequate on paper” while budgets were frozen and most volume went unmonitored. At Danske, customer, beneficial-owner and source-of-funds knowledge failed at branch level under an EU AML regime. Both cases were eventually detected and penalised, so they show control failure and delayed enforcement, not a regime without effect.

KYC-verified accounts can also sit on stolen or synthetic identities:

- **Austria:** the A-FIU reports accounts opened with forged Austrian passports carrying real, uninvolved persons' data and verified through qualified electronic signature or one-cent reference payments. One group held ten identities and 38 accounts at three banks. The A-FIU calls quality differences between outsourced remote-identification providers “enorm” (pp. 35–36).
- **United States:** FinCEN reports accounts opened with generative-AI or synthetic identity documents that passed identification and CDD checks, often detected only on later re-review. It gives no counts.
- **Crypto exchanges:** Eurojust describes a laundering service suspected of moving more than EUR 336 million through thousands of exchange accounts opened with stolen or purchased identities, with over 6,000 KYC records linked to mule accounts. These are allegations at the investigative stage. A 2024 journalistic test passed one exchange's document check with a USD 15 fake-ID image; this shows feasibility, not prevalence.

A KYC record therefore establishes whose identity was presented, not necessarily who controls the account. The same records also served as investigative material in the Eurojust case.

## Cost measurement

No official European series measures AML compliance cost. The Commission's 2021 impact assessment gives only a LexisNexis estimate for one medium-sized bank (USD 41–54 million a year), and Pol's global figures extrapolate the same commercial surveys. LexisNexis's Forrester-run survey (USD 206.1 billion globally, USD 85 billion in EMEA, 2023) is vendor-commissioned, covers sanctions and fraud as well as AML, and excludes Austria. Public-sector cost and customer-side burden are unmeasured.

## Benefits and counterevidence

The evidence against a categorical failure claim includes:

- substantial self-reported use of BSA reports within the six surveyed US federal agencies;
- identification of new subjects and links;
- real seizures and final confiscations;
- reporting responses to supervision without a measured average fall in the selected quality proxy;
- institutional reporting and market responses showing that controls can alter behaviour, without quantifying net prevention.
- measured asset-denial outputs: about EUR 20 million secured in Austria (2024), EUR 751.8 million through German immediate measures (2025), GBP 382.6 million denied through UK consent requests (2024-25), each concentrated in a few cases;
- rising Austrian ML convictions (152 in 2024) and 1,669 German court-stage feedback notices;
- detection and sanction of the TD Bank and Danske failures, and use of exchange KYC records in the Eurojust mule-account investigation.

The missing step is a defensible counterfactual: how much laundering, predicate crime and harm would have occurred without the relevant control, and at what total cost and rights burden?

## Side effects

De-risking and financial exclusion are recognised by the EBA and FATF itself. They are not fully quantified and may also arise from sanctions, profitability, capital, reputation or operational constraints. FATF's 2025 proportionality changes expressly encourage simplified measures at lower risk and attention to over-compliance.

Grey-listing illustrates de facto pressure. FATF does not legally demand blanket de-risking against grey-listed countries, yet the IMF working paper found a large associated capital-flow decline.

## Relevance to crypto and DAC8

General AML evidence can inform the public-interest and suitability analysis but cannot by itself establish the proportionality or effectiveness of a specific crypto or tax-reporting duty.

- KYC/CDD can create identity data and investigative chokepoints.
- That utility does not prove the incremental need for every field, customer category, retention period or onward transfer.
- DAC8 primarily serves tax transparency. AML asset-recovery or STR evidence is not an outcome measure for DAC8.
- Weak asset-recovery ratios likewise do not prove that DAC8 or crypto KYC is invalid or useless.
- The A-FIU finding on outsourced remote identification bears on the reliability of provider-held identity data that DAC8 reports reuse. It shows that such data can be wrong in documented cases; it does not measure an error rate in DAC8 or CASP records.
- VASPs filed 1,228 of 11,111 Austrian STRs in 2024 and 1.67 percent of UK SARs; these volumes do not show what share of crypto-related reports leads to an outcome.

## Claims to avoid

- “AML has been proven effective.”
- “AML has been proven ineffective.”
- “Ninety percent of reports are false.”
- “Less than one percent proves that 99 percent of AML fails.”
- “FATF ratings measure crime reduction.”
- “De-risking is caused only by AML.”
- “General AML evidence proves the effect of DAC8.”
- “AML costs a hundred times what it recovers” without Pol's caveats.
- “KYC identifies who controls an account.”
- “A-FIU dissemination rates show that 73 percent of reports are wrong.”
