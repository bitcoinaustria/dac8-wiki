# Wealth Inequality in the United States since 1913: Evidence from Capitalized Income Tax Data

Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, The Quarterly Journal of Economics 131(2), May 2016, pp. 519–578, doi:10.1093/qje/qjw004. Author-hosted journal PDF inspected (abstract; Sections III.B, IV.A–IV.D, IV.F).

Input data are individual income tax returns, which report annual totals per tax unit for nine capital-income categories (e.g. interest, dividends, rents, business profits). These are annual flow totals, not asset balances or itemised transactions. Section III.B.1 (pp. 532–533) maps each category to a household balance-sheet asset class and sets a capitalization factor equal to aggregate household wealth in that class divided by aggregate reported income. Example: in 2000 about $5 trillion of interest-bearing wealth against about $200 billion of reported taxable interest gives a factor of 25, i.e. an assumed 4% return. The factor was 37 for rents and 7 for partnership profits. From 1962 onward, wealth is imputed at individual level, assuming that everyone in an asset class has the same factor. Before 1962 the imputation is by income group. The method allocates known national totals across people and does not independently measure the level of any individual's wealth.

Results: the top 0.1% wealth share rose from 7% in 1978 to 22% in 2012 (abstract). The authors test the method against three micro sources. For foundations, capitalized income yields a 62.2% top-1% share against a true 62.8% (1985–2009, Section IV.C, p. 542). In the SCF (1989–2013), directly observed top 10/1/0.1% shares are 87.7/50.8/20.3%, compared with 89.0/48.8/20.7% when capitalized (Section IV.D, p. 544). Section IV.A (p. 541) derives an overestimation factor for idiosyncratic returns: with Pareto a = 1.5 and uniform returns on [0, 2r], top shares are overstated by 8.6%. Section IV.F (pp. 548–550) finds that top estate holders' interest rate was about 1.6 times the average in 2008–2012. Using that rate lowers top-0.1% fixed-income wealth by about 40% and the 2012 top-0.1% share from 22.0% to 18.7%.

Parallel to DAC8: this is the closest methodological precedent for turning annual, identity-linked, category-level totals reported by third parties into wealth estimates. The differences are substantial. The inputs are income yields on held stock, which have a stable relation to wealth within an asset class, whereas DAC8 reports gross purchase, sale and transfer flows, whose relation to year-end holdings depends on turnover and on holdings carried in from earlier years. The paper's validation targets population group shares and is anchored to macro balance-sheet totals. It does not validate the accuracy of an individual-level estimate.
