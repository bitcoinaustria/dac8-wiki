# Who Invests in Crypto? Wealth, Financial Constraints, and Risk Attitudes

Darren Aiello, Scott R. Baker, Tetyana Balyuk, Marco Di Maggio, Mark J. Johnson and Jason D. Kotter, NBER Working Paper 31856, November 2023 (not peer-reviewed as an NBER WP; also HBS WP 23-073). The NBER PDF was inspected: abstract, introduction pp. 1–5, Section I (Data), Section II pp. 13–16, Figure 6 (p. 39).

Inputs are de-identified bank and credit-card transaction data from a data aggregator covering millions of US users. Crypto purchases and sales are identified as fiat transfers between users' bank or card accounts and about 43 exchanges and platforms, mostly Coinbase (pp. 1, 7–8). Salary income, spending, overdrafts and gambling are derived from the same accounts. The paper observes fiat deposits to and withdrawals from exchanges. It does not observe exchange balances, crypto-to-crypto trades or on-chain holdings. About 18% of the sample are crypto investors (p. 8).

Results: users earning more than $75k account for about 73% of crypto transactions by count, and those earning less than $45k for about 11%. Dollar volume is concentrated in the right tail of the income distribution (p. 14). Median annual crypto investment for investors earning under $75k is typically below $1,000 and close to their traditional brokerage deposits. At $100–150k, crypto is about half of traditional after-tax investment flows. Above $150k, traditional flows are about five times the crypto flows (p. 16, Figure 6). Crypto flows respond to Bitcoin returns, income shocks, stimulus payments and household inflation exposure.

Parallel to DAC8: this is the closest found academic use of identity-linked fiat-to-exchange flow data, which resemble DAC8's fiat purchase and sale categories, to characterise investors. Annual flow amounts correlate with income class at group level, and counts and volumes are analysed separately. The paper treats flows as investment behaviour and does not estimate holdings or wealth from them. It gives no individual-level error figures and no validation of flow totals against balances. Early adopters who withdraw during booms show that flows and holdings can diverge.
