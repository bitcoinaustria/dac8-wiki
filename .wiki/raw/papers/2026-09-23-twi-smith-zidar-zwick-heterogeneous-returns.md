# Top Wealth in America: New Estimates Under Heterogeneous Returns

Matthew Smith, Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick, The Quarterly Journal of Economics 138(1), 2023, pp. 515–573, https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjac033. Author-hosted journal PDF inspected (abstract; Introduction pp. 516–520; Section on equity, p. 549; Table I, p. 551; Forbes discussion, p. 552).

Inputs are US administrative tax data. The authors build 3.2 billion source-owner observations linking taxable interest to its paying sources (2001–2016), firm-owner links for pass-through businesses, and dividends and realized gains (p. 518). The data remain annual flows per person and payer, not balances. The method keeps capitalization (W = βy) but lets returns vary by wealth group and asset class. The introduction (p. 516) notes that at low interest rates, small return differences δ produce large changes in the factor 1/(r+δ).

Quantified results: the fixed-income interest rate at the top is about 3.5 times the average (abstract), with a 2016 confidence interval of 2.8–4.3 (p. 519). 2016 returns rise from nearly 1% for the bottom 99.9% to 3.8% for the top 0.01% (p. 518). Table I (2016, p. 551) compares the baseline with equal returns. Top 1% share: 33.7% vs 38.9%. Top 0.1%: 15.7% vs 20.4%. Top 0.001%: 3.2% vs 5.1%. Average wealth of the top 0.001% is $1.02bn vs $1.63bn, a relative overstatement of about 59% under equal returns. Re-ranking matters: 17% of pass-through wealth accrues to people who report pass-through losses (p. 519), and footnote 3 says re-ranking plays a larger role than in earlier work. They also note that capitalizing equity flows "may miss some of the richest Americans, for whom the majority of capital gains are unrealized" (p. 549). The overall trend conclusion is unchanged.

Parallel to DAC8: this is the main quantified critique of inferring wealth from annual per-person flow totals. Even with income flows that are tied to holdings by construction, the flow-to-stock mapping error is large at the individual and top-group level unless external data on returns or composition are available. It shrinks only when estimates are aggregated and benchmarked to macro totals. DAC8 gross purchase and sale totals have no yield-like mapping to holdings at all, so the individual-level error would be expected to be at least as large. This is an inference, not a finding of the paper.
