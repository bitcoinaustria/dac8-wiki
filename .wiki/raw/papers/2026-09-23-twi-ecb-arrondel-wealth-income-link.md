# Wealth and Income in the Euro Area: Heterogeneity in Households' Behaviours?

Luc Arrondel, Muriel Roger, Frédérique Savignac (Banque de France). ECB Working Paper Series No 1709, August 2014 (Household Finance and Consumption Network). ISBN 978-92-899-1117-7; SSRN 2442272. I inspected the full PDF text: the abstract, the non-technical summary, Section 2.2 (descriptive statistics, p. 6-7) and the results on Figures 3-4.

Data: first wave of the Eurosystem Household Finance and Consumption Survey (HFCS), about 62,500 households in 15 euro-area countries, with household-level net wealth, gross income, earnings and inheritances. Method: descriptive correlations and a quintile cross-tabulation of household ranks, followed by a generalised ordered probit of wealth decile on income position, gifts and inheritances, age and other controls.

Figures (p. 6): "the correlation between net wealth and total income amounts to 0.33 in the Euro area", which is "lower for earnings (0.23)". By country, it is "less than 0.20 in Belgium and Malta", about 0.48 in Luxembourg, Italy and Portugal, and about 0.60 in Finland. Dispersion also differs: the third net-wealth quartile is 17 times the first, while for income the ratio is 2.8. The top 10% hold about 50% of net wealth but only 30% of gross income. Quintile cross-tab (p. 7, Figure 2): for the middle quintiles, only 20-25% of households share the same income and wealth quintile, which is close to the 20% expected under independence. At the extremes, 25-50% of the bottom income quintile are also in the bottom wealth quintile, and 35-65% of the top income quintile are in the top wealth quintile. The authors find "some high income households are poor in wealth ... while conversely some low income households are wealthy". Gifts and inheritances are a separate driver of wealth rank alongside income. Estimated probabilities of being in a given wealth decile given income decile vary widely: for example, the probability of being in the bottom wealth decile with bottom-decile income ranges from <20% (France) to >45% (Italy) (Figure 4).

Limits for the thesis: even *perfectly observed* annual income, which is a much fuller flow measure than DAC8's per-asset transaction totals, explains only about 10% of the cross-sectional variance in household net wealth (r = 0.33, r² of about 0.11). The link is close to random in the middle of the distributions, and it varies strongly by country, because wealth depends on inheritance, age and accumulated saving that flows do not reveal. Inferring stocks from flows at the individual level is therefore structurally noisy. The paper does NOT show that flows are useless: the association is stronger in the tails (top income and top wealth), which supports coarse flagging of extremes. It also concerns income flows, not crypto-asset transaction volumes, which may relate to holdings differently (e.g. through turnover and trading intensity).
