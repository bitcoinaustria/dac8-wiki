# Bridy, "Internet Payment Blockades"

Annemarie Bridy, Florida Law Review vol. 67, no. 5. The issue page reads "September 2015"; the repository cover cites "67 Fla. L. Rev. 1523 (2016)". PDF retrieved 20 October 2026 (the API copy covers the article's opening pages and notes).

## Exact quotation (pp. 1524–1525)

After Moneybookers (August 2010), "In December of that year, PayPal did the same, followed shortly thereafter by Visa and MasterCard." … "None of the participating payment processors was under court order to block payments to WikiLeaks, and there had been no legal process in either the United Kingdom or the United States finding Assange or WikiLeaks guilty of any crime." The blockade "resulted from a series of business decisions by corporate executives and their risk managers."

## Further statements, with their support

- Assange's claim that the blockade cost 95 percent of revenue (n. 4, The Guardian) is a party assertion reported by the author.
- Documents released by WikiLeaks in connection with a European Commission investigation are said to establish pressure by US lawmakers' staff on MasterCard and Visa (n. 8). The article treats this as established; it is not a court or Commission finding in this record.
- Amazon's termination of hosting "without any legal process or court order" (n. 15).

## Evidence class and limits

Secondary legal scholarship relying on press reports. It supports the narrow proposition that the processors acted without a court order. It does not establish government direction as a legal finding, any data disclosure about donors, or anything about tax reporting. "Blocked donations" is accurate for the payment-processing relationship; it does not mean individual donors' accounts were frozen.
