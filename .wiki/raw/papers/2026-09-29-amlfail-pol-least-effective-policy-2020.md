# Pol 2020: AML as a policy experiment

Ronald F. Pol (La Trobe Law School; also AMLassurance.com), *Policy Design and Practice* 3(1), 73–94 (received December 2018, accepted January 2020; open access, CC BY). The abstract (p. 73) states that the AML intervention "has less than 0.1 percent impact on criminal finances", that compliance costs exceed recovered criminal funds "more than a hundred times over", and that "the data are poorly validated and methodological inconsistencies rife, so findings cannot be definitive".

Method (section 5.3, pp. 80–81): the paper expressly lacks an official outcome measure and adopts the UN interception "success rate" (confiscated or seized proceeds divided by estimated criminal proceeds) as an "imperfect" interim proxy for harm reduction. Section 6.1 (pp. 82–83) re-computes UNODC 2011: USD 3.1 billion seized over USD 2.1 trillion proceeds in 2009 gives 0.15 percent; halving for Europol's seizure-to-confiscation ratio gives 0.07 percent. For Europe it uses Europol 2016 (about EUR 1.2 billion confiscated of at least EUR 110 billion revenue from selected markets, 1.1 percent) and argues the denominator is understated. Sections 6.2–6.3 (pp. 83–85) extrapolate global confiscations of about USD 2.7–4.5 billion against perhaps USD 2.9 trillion proceeds (0.09–0.16 percent) and adopt a rounded USD 3 billion. Section 6.4 (pp. 85–86) extrapolates LexisNexis survey costs by GDP share: about USD 156 billion (EUR 144 billion) for the EU and USD 304 billion (0.38 percent of world GDP) globally, and cites a Thomson Reuters figure implying USD 1.28 trillion. Hence "about $3 billion of an estimated $3 trillion" intercepted (0.1 percent) against more than USD 300 billion in compliance cost, and for Europe cost about 120 times recoveries. Section 6.6 (p. 87) cites industry tallies of AML penalties of USD 4.3 billion (2018) and USD 8.1 billion (2019).

Author caveats at the claim: "These figures are far from definitive"; "the apparent precision ... is illusory" (p. 85); compliance costs are "poorly validated" (p. 86); public-sector costs are unknown (section 6.5). The paper does not measure deterrence, displacement, prevention at onboarding or investigative use; the confiscation ratio is a recovery proxy, not a total-effect estimate. The cost side rests on commercial surveys (LexisNexis, Thomson Reuters) extrapolated by GDP, and the author has a commercial AML-assurance affiliation. The paper is peer-reviewed and widely cited but is an argumentative policy analysis, not a causal evaluation.

Transfer limits: supports the narrower claim that AML recovery is small relative to modelled proceeds and that no official cost–benefit analysis exists. It does not establish that AML or KYC has no preventive effect, and it says nothing specific about crypto, DAC8 tax reporting or Austrian costs. The 100x multiple compares non-comparable, extrapolated inputs and should be quoted only with those caveats.
