# Danske Bank Estonia

DOJ press release 22-1342, 13 December 2022: Danske Bank A/S pleaded guilty to one count of conspiracy to commit bank fraud and agreed to criminal forfeiture of USD 2.059 billion, with about USD 850 million credited for payments to the SEC and Danish authorities; the SEC settlement was about USD 413 million (civil penalty USD 178.6 million). Admitted facts (2008–2016): the Estonian branch's non-resident portfolio (NRP) attracted customers by letting them move large sums "with little, if any, oversight"; employees helped customers use shell companies to obscure ownership; the branch processed USD 160 billion through US banks for the NRP; by at least February 2014 the bank knew of highly suspicious transactions and inadequate AML procedures but misrepresented its AML program, transaction monitoring and customer risk to US correspondent banks.

Bruun & Hjejle, "Report on the Non-Resident Portfolio at Danske Bank's Estonian branch", 19 September 2018 (87 pages; executive summary items 4–13, pp. 6–7): about 15,000 customers investigated and about 9.5 million payments; the flow over 2007–2015 was about EUR 200 billion. In 2014 the group realised AML procedures had been "manifestly insufficient and inadequate" and that "all control functions (or lines of defence) had failed", including insufficient knowledge of customers, beneficial owners and source of funds, and mainly manual screening. Of about 6,200 customers examined, "the vast majority" were deemed suspicious; the branch had historically filed SARs on 653 NRP customers. The report cautions that there is "no accurate estimate" of the suspicious share of the flow, that "suspicious" does not itself implicate criminal activity, and that proving laundering requires more information than a bank possesses.

Evidence class: an official plea summary (admitted facts) plus a bank-commissioned law-firm investigation. The EUR 200 billion is total non-resident flow, not proven laundering; the frequent "EUR 200 billion laundered" shorthand is wrong. The case shows a failure of branch-level CDD and group oversight within an EU AML regime, recognised at group level in 2014 after a whistleblower and internal audit, and later sanctioned. The Commission's 2021 AML impact assessment cites the case in its problem definition as "EUR 200 billion in suspicious transactions", itself a loose reading of the flow figure.

Transfer limits: supports that KYC obligations on paper did not prevent massive high-risk flows through an EU bank for years. It concerns corporate non-resident banking and correspondent access, not retail crypto users, and does not quantify the effect of CDD generally.
