# The association between gambling and financial, social and health outcomes in big financial data

Naomi Muggleton, Paula Parpart, Philip Newall, David Leake, John Gathergood, Neil Stewart, *Nature Human Behaviour* 5: 319–326 (March 2021). The full article PDF was read, from a third-party-hosted copy of the published version.

**Data and method.** The study used three anonymised samples from Lloyds Banking Group (LBG): 102,195 active customers in 2018, all 6.5 million customers active in each month of 2013 (followed to 2019), and 101,151 customers active from 2012 to 2018. Gambling was identified from the bank's merchant categories (bookmakers, casinos, lotteries), expressed as a share of monthly income, and related to 31 outcomes. Some variables come from transaction metadata. For example, night-time spending between 1:00 and 5:00 is derived from timestamps. LBG's internal Privacy Risk and Impact Assessment Committee approved the research, which relied on customer consent in account terms. The study was funded by LBG and ESRC.

**Findings.** Higher gambling is associated with overdraft use, missed payments, payday loans and less spending on self-care, fitness, social activities, education and hobbies. A 10-point increase in gambling's share corresponds to an 11.5% increase in "nights awake" and a 9% reduction in social activities. In the 6.5 million sample, higher gambling is associated with later unemployment and disability-payment receipt. In Cox models, heavy gambling is associated with about one-third higher 5-year mortality. For example, among 44-year-old women, gambling 30% of expenditure raises the chance of death from 50 to 69 in 10,000 (factor 1.37). The authors state they do not establish causality. Cash gambling and payments via intermediaries such as PayPal are not captured.

**Evidence class and DAC8 transfer.** Peer-reviewed and very large-scale. It shows that a bank's own categorised card data expose a behaviour with health relevance (gambling) as well as sleep-pattern proxies and disability-benefit receipt. It measures population associations, not the accuracy of individual labels. It depends on merchant category and timestamp for each transaction, which the DAC8 report lacks; it transfers to provider-held records. It also shows that bank data science teams already derive such variables internally.
