# TD Bank 2024 plea and FinCEN penalty

US Department of Justice press release 24-1286, 10 October 2024: TD Bank N.A. pleaded guilty to conspiring to fail to maintain a BSA-compliant AML program, fail to file accurate CTRs and launder money; its US holding company pleaded to related program and CTR counts. DOJ penalty USD 1,886,945,780.40 (forfeiture USD 452.4 million, fine USD 1.43 billion), three-year monitor; coordinated resolutions with the Federal Reserve, OCC and FinCEN. FinCEN's same-day release assessed a USD 1.3 billion penalty, "the largest penalty against a depository institution" in Treasury/FinCEN history, and a four-year monitorship.

Admitted facts as described by DOJ: from January 2014 to October 2023 the bank had "long-term, pervasive, and systemic deficiencies"; senior executives enforced a "flat cost paradigm" keeping budgets flat despite rising profits and risk; the program "appeared adequate on paper"; transaction monitoring "remained effectively static" from 2014 through 2022, with no new scenarios; the bank did not automatically monitor domestic ACH, most checks and other types, "resulting in 92% of total transaction volume going unmonitored from Jan. 1, 2018, to April 12, 2024", about USD 18.3 trillion. Three networks moved more than USD 670 million in 2019–2023 (one over USD 470 million in cash via nominee accounts; employees received gift cards; five employees issued ATM cards for a network laundering about USD 39 million). FinCEN found willful failure to file SARs on thousands of transactions totalling about USD 1.5 billion, inadequate P2P (Zelle) monitoring and known funnel-account activity.

Evidence class: government press releases summarising admitted facts in plea agreements and consent orders (the plea documents themselves were not read). This is a documented institution-level failure, not a system-level effectiveness estimate. It is two-sided: the failure went on for years under supervision, but the case was ultimately detected, prosecuted and penalised, over two dozen individuals were charged, and the regime supplied the legal basis.

Transfer limits: supports that formal KYC/AML programs can coexist with largely unmonitored activity and insider complicity, so compliance on paper is not an effectiveness measure. It is a US bank case with no bearing on crypto-asset reporting or DAC8 except as an analogy about control quality; it is not evidence that retail-customer CDD is useless.
