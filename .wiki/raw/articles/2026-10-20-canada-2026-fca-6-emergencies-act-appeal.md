## Holdings relevant here

- Para. 416 (adverse to the first-instance finding): s. 8 "protects privacy rights—it does not protect against restrictions on the enjoyment of property"; frozen-account holders' "confidential financial information was not disclosed to anyone", so s. 2(1) did not authorise searches or seizures.
- Para. 417: s. 5 required institutions to disclose private financial information about designated persons to the RCMP or CSIS; s. 6 allowed governments to disclose information to institutions.
- Paras. 451–458: searches under ss. 5–6 were unreasonable. They rested on "potentially unfounded, subjective beliefs" (para. 452), concerned the "biographical core of personal information" (para. 453), had only a "reason to believe" standard, no review mechanism and no prior authorisation (para. 455), and relied on news or internet reports, "troubling in the extreme" (para. 456).
- Paras. 469–474: the most serious defect was warrantless sharing of personal banking information with the RCMP and CSIS (para. 470). Less impairing options included use limits, court leave, a reliable information source, a higher suspicion threshold, review and prior authorisation (paras. 471–473). The s. 8 infringement was not justified under s. 1 (para. 474).
- Paras. 506–508: the Attorney General's appeals and the CCLA/CCF cross-appeals were dismissed; the Nagle/CFN appeal and the interlocutory appeals were dismissed with costs.

## Limits

Canadian Charter authority on emergency information sharing between banks and police. It does not hold that account freezing itself breached s. 8, and it is not tax, EU or Austrian law.
