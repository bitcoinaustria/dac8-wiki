# C-252/21, Meta Platforms and Others (General terms of use of a social network)

Judgment of the Court (Grand Chamber), 4 July 2023, ECLI:EU:C:2023:537. The official English text was read in full (HTML manifestation from the EU Publications Office Cellar, CELEX 62021CJ0252).

**Holdings on Article 9(1) GDPR (Question 2(a)).** Para. 68: the test is whether the processed data "allow information falling within one of the categories … to be revealed", whether it concerns the user or any other natural person. Para. 69: the prohibition applies whether or not the revealed information is correct and whether or not the controller aims to obtain sensitive information. Para. 70: the prohibition applies "irrespective of its stated purpose". Paras. 71–72: the processing at issue was the collection of data on visits to websites and apps, including information entered when registering or placing online orders, linked to users' accounts. Whether this actually reveals sensitive information is for the national court. Visits alone may suffice in some cases. Para. 73 answers the question, and the operative part adopts that answer. Para. 89: where a dataset containing sensitive and non-sensitive items is collected en bloc and the items cannot be separated at collection, processing of the whole set is prohibited if it contains at least one sensitive item and no Article 9(2) derogation applies.

**Related holdings.** Paras. 76–79 hold that Article 9(2) is to be interpreted strictly, and that visiting sensitive sites does not "manifestly make public" the data under Article 9(2)(e). Para. 117: users cannot reasonably expect personalised-advertising processing without consent. Para. 118 describes the processing as "particularly extensive", relating to "potentially unlimited data", which "may give rise to the feeling that his or her private life is being continuously monitored".

**Evidence class and limits.** This is binding EU case law (Grand Chamber) and extends C-184/20 (indirect revelation) to linked behavioural and purchase data processed by a private platform. The Court sets a legal test ("allows … to be revealed"); it does not find as fact that particular purchases reveal particular traits. The case concerns a private controller relying on consent and Article 6(1)(b)/(f), not a statutory public-law tax obligation under Article 6(1)(c)/(e). For DAC8 the transferable principle is this. Where provider-held crypto records (counterparties, merchants, timestamps) allow sensitive information to be revealed, Article 9 applies to them regardless of the controller's purpose. It must be shown separately whether the DAC8 annual aggregates themselves allow such revelation. The aggregates contain no counterparty or merchant data, so that showing is harder.
