# OGH 7 Ob 95/24g (28 August 2024)

Oberster Gerichtshof, decision of 28 August 2024, 7 Ob 95/24g, ECLI:AT:OGH0002:2024:0070OB00095.24G.0828.000. The appeal was from OLG Linz 11 R 7/24i and LG Salzburg 14 Cg 54/23p. The decision type is "Zurückweisung mangels erheblicher Rechtsfrage": a rejection that decides no new principle and defers to the lower courts' assessment of the individual case. Commented in ZFR 2025/8 and EvBl 2025/111.

**Facts (paras 1–2).** After clicking an SMS link disguised as a parcel notice and entering his banking user number, the customer received a call from a fraudster. The fraudster had him confirm, in his app, the fraudster's own online-banking login. He then told him that funds had to be "recovered". The customer released a EUR 20,000 foreign transfer, which the bank's fraud-transaction monitoring stopped as atypical. He then released four more pre-filled transfers (EUR 9,000, 9,000, 3,950 and 5,700) with both app steps. These were not stopped and could not be recalled.

**Holdings.**

- Para 10: because the claimant himself treated the orders as authorised by him, "[e]ine Haftung gemäß §§ 67, 68 ZaDiG 2018 kommt damit keinesfalls in Betracht" (liability under §§ 67, 68 ZaDiG 2018 is out of the question).
- Paras 12–13: the scope of the bank's protective duties is a case-by-case question. The lower courts' finding of no breach, given transaction monitoring and prior phishing warnings, did not need correction.
- Para 15: the appeal court's alternative ground stands. Even if the bank had breached a duty, the customer's conduct, which the appeal court classed as grossly negligent (para 6), would push it so far into the background that it could be disregarded.
- Para 16: "Dass ein betrügerischer Zahlungsvorgang gerade auch ein autorisierter Zahlungsvorgang sein kann" (that a fraudulent payment can also be an authorised one) is the reason for monitoring already-authorised payments. The court declined a CJEU reference on the relation between "unauthorised" and "fraudulent" in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/389, Article 2.

**Use.** This is Austrian supreme-court authority, though a rejection decision rather than a leading case. It is **adverse** to any claim that Austrian bank customers are protected against social-engineering losses. Where the victim personally releases the payment, the ZaDiG 2018 refund does not apply, and recovery depends on contractual duties of care, which were not breached here. For the bearer-asset comparison, the bank/self-custody gap narrows sharply for manipulated transfers. What remains on the bank side is fraud monitoring, which stopped EUR 20,000 here, and possible recall. No Austrian decision on physical coercion (duress) was found.
