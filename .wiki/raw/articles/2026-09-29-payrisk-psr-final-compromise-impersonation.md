# PSR final compromise text (Council doc. 8221/26)

Sources: Council document ST 8221/26 (LIMITE, made public), the final compromise text of the Regulation on payment services in the internal market (procedure 2023/0210(COD)). Council 'I' item note ST 8220/26 of 17 April 2026. European Parliament Legislative Observatory procedure file 2023/0210(COD), read 29 September 2026.

**Status (not enacted law).**

- The note (points 4–8) records the final political trilogue of 26 November 2025, a silent consultation ending 25 March 2026 with no objection, and a request that Coreper approve the texts "with a view to reaching an agreement at second reading".
- The Observatory lists ECON approval of the agreed text (PE787.675) on 5 May 2026. The stage is "Awaiting Council's 1st reading position", and the forecast is an "Indicative plenary sitting date" of 14 December 2026.
- No Official Journal publication was found. Law-firm forecasts of OJ publication in mid-2026 have not materialised.
- Article 112: application 21 months after entry into force, with some exceptions.

**Impersonation fraud (Article 59, pp. 292–293; recitals 80–81b, pp. 74–78).**

- Article 59(1): where a consumer "was manipulated by a third party pretending to be the consumer's payment service provider using communication channels attributed to" that provider, and this "gave rise to subsequent fraudulent authorised payment transactions", the provider "shall refund the consumer the full amount". The condition is that the consumer notified the provider and "reported the fraud to the police" without undue delay.
- Article 59(2): the provider decides within 15 business days.
- Article 59(3)–(4): no refund for fraud or gross negligence. The burden is on the provider, which must first invite the consumer's account of events.
- Recital 80a: the refund covers misuse of the provider's email, telephone number, website or app, including cloned sites.
- Article 59a: data exchange with hosting and electronic-communications providers. Telecom providers must take measures against caller-ID and email spoofing, but recital 81b rules out "generalised and indiscriminate monitoring".

**Unauthorised transactions (Article 56(1), p. 288).** The provider refunds by the end of the next business day unless it has "objectively justified reasons for suspecting" intentional or grossly negligent breach of Article 52 duties, communicated in writing, or payer fraud. This is broader than the PSD2 Article 73 exception that AG Rantos read narrowly in C-70/25.

**Crypto scope.**

- Recital 29 and the definition of funds: e-money tokens (EMTs) are included.
- Article 2(2)(a1) (p. 158) excludes "payment transactions made exclusively in electronic money tokens directly from the payer to the payee, without any intermediary intervention". Recital 29b explains that this covers transfers "between two self-hosted addresses" but not custodial-to-self-hosted payments.
- Recital 29a excludes certain CASP exchange and trading activity with EMTs.
- Searches of the text for "coerc", "duress", "threat" and "violence" returned nothing.

**Use.** The text is a legislative choice, not a holding, and it confirms two points:

1. Manipulated payments that the payer releases are "authorised". They become refundable only in the narrow PSP-impersonation case.
2. Other authorised push payment fraud (romance, investment, relative-impersonation "Enkeltrick") and physical coercion stay outside any refund right.

The explicit self-hosted EMT exclusion supports the bearer-asset contrast for self-custody. The contrast narrows for impersonation fraud once the PSR applies, which is not before roughly 2028.
